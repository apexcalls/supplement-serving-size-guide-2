Supplement Serving Sizes: How to Compare Products Fairly

Serving size is the most overlooked number on a supplement label. Shoppers compare prices per bottle, doses per capsule and ingredient lists — but they rarely stop to check what a serving actually is. Because serving sizes are set by manufacturers rather than by law, two products can look very different on price and dose while being nearly identical once the serving is taken into account.

The first step in any fair comparison is to find the serving size on the Supplement Facts panel and work out what a single serving costs. Divide the bottle price by the number of servings, not by the number of capsules: a bottle of 60 capsules with a two-capsule serving contains 30 servings, and its real price is double what the per-capsule figure suggests. This single calculation clears up most of the confusion that supplement pricing creates.

Next, check the amount of active ingredient per serving, not per capsule. A capsule that contains 250 mg of an ingredient, taken twice a day, delivers the same daily amount as a single 500 mg capsule — but the labels will look different, and the two-capsule product may appear weaker or more expensive depending on how you read it. Always compare daily amounts, not single-capsule amounts.

Also consider why serving sizes differ. Sometimes the difference reflects the form of the ingredient: a standardised extract is more concentrated than a raw powder, so a smaller serving can deliver the same compound. Sometimes it reflects the manufacturer's design: a product intended to be taken with food may split its dose across two capsules to improve tolerance. And sometimes it reflects marketing: a smaller serving makes a bottle last longer on paper and the price per bottle look lower.

Serving sizes also change over time. When a brand reformulates — switching supplier, changing an extract's standardisation, or adding an ingredient — it may adjust the serving to keep the dose consistent or to keep the price consistent while the dose changes. The new label will show the new serving size, but it will not explain the change. For a product you buy regularly, compare the new label with the old one and look for changes in the amounts, not just the serving count.

Serving size is a manufacturer-defined number, not a measure of quality. Normalise it before you compare anything else, and the labels will start to give up their secrets.
